How these numbers are calculated

For a drawing with N plays, the number of tickets that hit a prize tier with probability p follows a binomial distribution, which for these tiny probabilities is very well approximated by a Poisson distribution with mean N·p.

Plays. Each drawing's play count is estimated from its seven fixed-prize tiers combined: N = (total winners across those tiers) ÷ (sum of their probabilities). That's the maximum-likelihood estimate for pooled Poisson counts. It is a weighted average of the per-tier estimates, not their sum, and the common tiers dominate because their counts are the most precise. The two lifetime tiers are left out so they aren't used to predict themselves.

Expected winners. Estimated plays × the official probability for that prize. The probabilities in the last two columns treat the actual count as a draw from a Poisson distribution with that expected mean.

Caveats. Players don't choose numbers uniformly (birthdays, patterns, "lucky" numbers), so a single night's winner counts swing more than a pure random model predicts, depending on which numbers came up. That's visible below: the per-tier estimates disagree by more than their statistical intervals. Uneven number choice doesn't change how many winners to expect on average (every drawing is uniformly random), but it does make the actual count more variable than the Poisson model assumes. So the probabilities above, and the plays interval, overstate how surprising a result is and how precise the estimate is. Read them as rough guides. The top prize is also pari-mutuel per the official rules. Odds are the official published odds for a $$5 play.
